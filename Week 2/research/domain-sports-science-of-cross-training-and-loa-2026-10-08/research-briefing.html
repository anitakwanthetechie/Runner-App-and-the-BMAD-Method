Executive summary

Yes, the app can give evidence-based answers to both of your swim questions. They are more nuanced than "swimming counts" or "swimming doesn't count".

  1. A swim in place of a skipped run (your first question):
    • One skipped run costs no measurable race fitness. VO₂max starts to fall only after about 10–14 days of no running at all [8][9][7].
    • Swimming is the weakest stand-in for running. In the only trial in runners, swimming added to a running plan improved a 3.2 km time trial by 13.2 s. Extra running improved it by 26.4 s, and the control group by 5.4 s [1]. So a swim is worth roughly half a run for running performance, or about a third once the control group's gain is subtracted. It mostly trains swimming [2][3].
    • Deep-water running and cycling are better substitutes. Deep-water running maintained running performance for 4–6 weeks in trained runners [13][14][15].
    • Missing ingredient: to keep fitness while running less, keep some hard running sessions and cut volume, not intensity [6][11][12].
  2. Three extra swims a week on top of all your runs (your second question): they help general fitness, add only a little running gain, are unlikely to hurt, and count as training load, not rest [1][22][30]. The app wording is in R2.
  3. Guardrails for the MVP:
    • Mostly easy, not strict 80/20. Polarized and pyramidal training perform the same [31][32]. The real risk is easy runs drifting into the middle zone [33].
    • Spike warning: warn when one run is more than 10% longer than your longest run of the past 30 days [37]. A secondary flag catches weekly distance up more than 30% over 2 weeks [36][38]. Both are labeled "early evidence". The weekly "10% rule" is unproven [35].
    • See a professional: a list of bone-stress red flags, such as pinpoint bone pain, pain at rest or at night, and groin or hip pain [43][66].

Biggest caveats: - Wrist heart rate can't be trusted in the pool. Readings run 9–44 bpm low [48][49]. Swim load must come from your effort rating (session RPE), or from a chest strap or goggle sensor. - Several rules rest on one group or one small trial (the spike warning, the swim-vs.-run trial, and the pain rule). The app's "Why?" text should say so.

Decision for you: where swim cross-training goes in MoSCoW. Suggested: SHOULD (sprint 2), with R3 and R4 in the MUST load model (see Recommendations).

1. Swim → run carry-over

Swimming keeps general aerobic fitness but transfers only partly to running. A single swapped run is harmless, and weeks of swaps need a different substitute or a gradual return.

Detraining (how fast fitness fades): - Stopping completely: - After 14 days without training, distance runners' VO₂max fell about 4%, but running economy didn't change [8]. - Female middle-distance runners showed no change at day 5. Their VO₂max dropped by day 10, and their 2,400 m time was 21.5 s slower by day 15 [9]. - In another study, VO₂max fell 7% in the first 21 days [7]. Verified Verified across three labs. - Keep intensity, cut volume. Fitness is kept when intensity is maintained and frequency is cut only moderately. Volume can drop a lot [6]. Runners who cut volume by 70% but kept some hard work held their 5 km time for 3 weeks [10]. Cutting duration by up to two-thirds kept VO₂max [11], but cutting intensity lost it [12]. Verified Verified.

How well swimming transfers: - Trial in runners. In 30 trained runners over 8 weeks, adding 10% extra load as swimming improved 3.2 km time by 13.2 s. The same extra load as running improved it by 26.4 s, and the control group by 5.4 s [1]. Treadmill VO₂max and running economy didn't change in any group. Only the extra-running group improved its lactate threshold pace [1]. Verified Verified. One small RCT, testing swims added to running rather than replacing it. - An earlier draft of this research said added swimming gave no running gain. Checking the original paper overturned that. - Specificity. Ten weeks of swim training raised swimming VO₂max but not running VO₂max [2]. A review concludes that swim training "may result in minimum transfer" and that cross-training "never exceed[s]" sport-specific training [3]. Modeling in elite triathletes found swimming "highly specific" [4]. Verified Verified. Three independent groups. - Moderately trained people may still benefit from a different activity [6]. A university thesis reports that swim training improved running in untrained people [64]. Medium confidence Single source, seen only as a search snippet. - No replacement trial exists. No retrieved study had runners replace runs with swims.

Better substitutes: - Deep-water running maintained VO₂max, threshold, and running economy for 6 weeks [13]. Four weeks of deep-water running alone maintained 5 km time [14]. Water running and cycling both maintained 2-mile time over 6 weeks, with a small VO₂max dip in all groups [15]. A systematic review finds deep-water running has effects on aerobic fitness similar to land training [60]. Verified Verified across three labs. - A 2026 meta-analysis found no clear difference between cycling-only and running-only training, but warns the evidence doesn't prove the two are interchangeable [16]. - Ranking: deep-water running ≈ cycling > swimming. This is an inference: no head-to-head trial compares them with swimming.

Swim-to-run conversions: - Ratios like "1.5 km swim ≈ 6 km run" (4:1) come from coaches, with no studies cited [17]. No peer-reviewed validation of any swim-to-run equivalence was found. Unverified Unverified: absence of evidence.

Bones and tendons: - Swimming gives a weak bone-building stimulus compared with weight-bearing sport [18]. - With a fully unloaded leg, tendon collagen production fell about 55% by day 10 [19], and Achilles tendon stiffness fell 17% after 4 weeks [20]. Those studies used complete unloading, so they show the worst case. Swimming plus daily walking unloads far less. - Implication: after weeks of swim-only training, ease back into running.

2. Extra swims vs. rest

Extra swims are a small aerobic plus, not a running booster. Easy swims are about as good as rest for recovery. All swims count toward total load.

Added cross-training: - Added swimming gave about half the running gain of added running, or about a third net of the control group (see §1) [1]. - In another study, runners who increased training through cross-training or through running only improved 5 km time to a similar extent. The cross-training mode wasn't confirmed and may have been cycling [21]. Medium confidence Single source. Medium-low confidence.

Active recovery vs. rest: - A meta-analysis of 99 studies found active recovery reduced muscle soreness (DOMS) with a moderate-to-large effect (SMD −0.94), but only shortly after exercise. It had no effect on perceived fatigue [22]. Verified Verified. - A swim 10 hours after hard intervals improved next-day run endurance in 9 triathletes. That swim was hard (20 × 100 m), not easy [23]. Medium confidence Single source. Not replicated. - No retrieved study showed an easy swim harms recovery.

Overreaching and overtraining: - The ECSS/ACSM consensus defines three stages [24][25]: - Functional overreaching: a short performance dip that ends in improvement. - Non-functional overreaching: recovery takes weeks to months. - Overtraining syndrome: months or more, and diagnosed only after illness is ruled out. - No single marker meets all the criteria [24]. Key symptoms are fatigue, performance decline, and mood disturbance [24]. Verified Verified. - What to monitor. Self-report measures respond to load "with superior sensitivity and consistency" compared with objective measures [27]. In recreational runners, nightly heart rate, "readiness to train", and an HR-to-power index best separated overreached runners [26]. Verified Verified that the two kinds of signal work together. The claim that a questionnaire beats HRV was disputed, because neither replaces the other. HRV evidence is in §4.

Rest days: - No peer-reviewed rule sets the number of rest days for recreational runners, and none says whether a swim day counts as rest. Unverified Unverified: absence of evidence. Rest-day advice in the app is practice-based.

Energy (REDs): - REDs (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport) is health and performance harm from not eating enough for the training done. It affects women and men [29]. - In 109 recreational women, each extra weekly hour of exercise multiplied the odds of being at risk of under-fueling by 1.13 [30]. Medium confidence Single source. Cross-sectional association, questionnaire-screened risk, women only, counts all exercise hours. - Missed periods and bone stress injuries are screening indicators [29]. Verified Partly verified. The full clinical checklist wasn't retrieved.

3. Safety guardrails for runners

Coach "mostly easy" and watch single-run spikes. Send runners with bone-stress red flags to a professional. Taper 2–3 weeks. The taper evidence feeds the "Taper Jail" race-week feature.

Intensity distribution (80/20): - Polarized vs. pyramidal. An individual-data network meta-analysis (13 studies, 348 athletes) found no difference in VO₂max or time-trial performance [31]. A subgroup hint favored pyramidal for recreational athletes' VO₂max. An independent meta-analysis (17 studies) found a small polarized advantage for VO₂peak only in highly trained athletes, and no difference in recreational athletes [32]. Verified Verified. The pyramidal hint is low-certainty. - Middle-zone drift is the real risk. In 30 recreational runners, polarized training beat a threshold-heavy plan for 10 km time (−5.0% vs. −3.6%) [33], although the difference between groups wasn't statistically significant. Medium confidence Single source. Small RCT. Low-medium confidence. A trial in recreational marathoners is under way [65]. - How to count matters. Counting by session labels triples the apparent hard share compared with counting minutes in zone [34]. The app must state which method it uses. Medium confidence Single source, in elite skiers.

Mileage ramp: - Single-run spikes. In 5,205 runners with 588,000 sessions, a run longer than the longest run of the past 30 days raised injury hazard: - 10–30% longer: HRR 1.64 - 30–100% longer: HRR 1.52 - more than 100% longer: HRR 2.28

Weekly change wasn't associated with injury, and a high ACWR was linked to lower risk (HRR 0.75) [37]. Verified Verified figures. One research group, self-reported injuries, 78% male, mean age 46. Not replicated. The 10% is a category edge, not a tested cut-off. - Weekly 10% rule: unproven. - A 10%-rule program didn't reduce injuries in 532 novices (20.8% vs. 20.3%) [35]. - Increasing weekly distance by more than 30% over 2 weeks showed a non-significant trend toward more overuse injuries (HR 1.59, p = 0.07) [36]. Verified Verified, after correction: an earlier draft called this an association. - Increases of 20–60% raised short-term risk in half-marathoners [38]. Medium confidence Single source. Wide confidence interval.

Injury baseline: - Novices get 17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours, compared with 7.7 for recreational runners [39]. Previous injury is the strongest risk factor [40][41]. Verified Verified. This is why R6 and R9 should weigh more for novices and for runners with a past injury.

Pain rules and red flags: - Pain-monitoring model. Pain up to 5/10 during activity is acceptable if it settles by the next morning and doesn't build week to week. In 38 patients with Achilles tendinopathy, this model gave results similar to 6 weeks of rest [42]. Medium confidence Single source. Tendon pain only: it is not validated for bone pain. - Bone stress red flags: - pinpoint bone tenderness (the "most significant" finding) [43] - pain that comes earlier in each run or builds during it - pain at rest or at night - pain on hopping - local swelling - a new limp

Groin, hip, or front-of-thigh pain suggests a femoral neck injury and needs urgent referral. High-risk sites are the femoral neck, the navicular (midfoot), the base of the 5th metatarsal, and the front of the shin [43]. The symptom list also draws on a clinician's guide for runners [66]. A 2025 international consensus exists, but its diagnostic statements weren't retrieved [44]. Medium confidence Single clinical review plus a clinician blog. Medium confidence: the retrieved review text didn't show every symptom listed.

Taper: - The best taper is about 2 weeks: cut volume by 41–60% and keep intensity and frequency [45][46]. - Data on 158,000 recreational marathoners favor a strict, progressive taper of up to 3 weeks [47]. Verified Verified. The marathon data are observational. - "The hay is in the barn" has no source that quantifies when new training stops paying off. Unverified Unverified.

4. Measurement validity

Use effort ratings for swims and the runner's own measured max heart rate for runs. Show heart-rate variability only as a personal trend.

Wrist heart rate: - In the pool: wrist readings in water were 17–30% off (Garmin −44 bpm, Polar −14 bpm on average) [48]. An older wristwatch had limits of agreement of −53 to +34 bpm, while a goggle (temple) sensor did well [49]. A third study found the wrist far worse than the temple [62]. Verified Verified, but small studies. - Apple Watch: its only water result (MAE 0.9 bpm) came from an aquatic treadmill, not lap swimming [50]. No pool validation of current Apple, Garmin, or Huawei watches was found. - Running: wrist heart rate is reasonable. Apple Watch SE error was 3.1%, but individual accuracy varied widely [51].

Effort ratings (RPE): - Session RPE is valid and reliable across sports and levels [53]. In swimmers it correlated with heart-rate load at r = 0.55–0.94 [52]. Verified Verified. - Sleep loss raises RPE for the same work (SMD 0.51 overall, 0.39 in athletes) [54], so a high rating on an easy day may reflect poor sleep. Athletes often sleep less than 7 hours a night, and the consensus advises an individual approach to sleep need [28]. - No source was found on heat or mood effects.

Max heart rate and zones: - Every age formula has about ±10 bpm individual error (220 − age RMSE 9.8, Tanaka 9.2, in 4,043 runners) [55]. Women's measured max sits below the formulas [56]. Verified Verified. - In 1,411 runners, VT1 (the top of the easy zone) sat at 85% of max heart rate, and VT2 at 94% [58]. Medium confidence Single source, though large. This contradicts the 65–75% often quoted by vendors. - The 3-zone model (easy below VT1, moderate between thresholds, hard above VT2) is the simplest practical model [59].

Resting heart rate and HRV from the watch: - Apple Watch resting heart rate matched a chest strap almost exactly. Its HRV had 29% error and is fit only for personal trends [57]. Verified Verified. - Interpreting HRV needs context from how the runner feels: in one study it rose both with overreaching and with improved performance [63].

Glossary

Plain-English terms the app can show runners, followed by the statistics used in this report. Definitions are plain-language paraphrases. The sources show where each term is used, not a verbatim definition.

Term Plain-English definition Source
VO₂max The most oxygen your body can use per minute in all-out effort: the size of your aerobic engine. [3][7]
Running economy How much oxygen you need to run at a given pace. Lower is better. [8][13]
Lactate threshold / VT1, VT2 VT1 is where breathing first picks up and the easy zone ends. VT2 is where effort becomes hard to sustain. [58][59]
Specificity Your body adapts mostly to the exact activity you train. [2][3]
Cross-training Training in a different sport to support your main one. [1]
Deep-water running Running motion in deep water with a float belt, feet off the bottom. [13][14]
Detraining Losing fitness when training stops or drops sharply. [5]
Session RPE Your 0–10 rating of how hard a session felt, multiplied by its minutes, gives a load score. [53]
Training load (internal vs. external) External is the work done (distance, time). Internal is how hard your body found it (heart rate, effort). [61]
Polarized / pyramidal (TID) Ways of splitting training across intensities (training intensity distribution). Polarized: mostly easy, some hard, little in between. Pyramidal: mostly easy, less moderate, least hard. [31][59]
Functional overreaching A short planned dip in performance that bounces back better after rest. [24]
Overtraining syndrome A long-lasting breakdown in performance and health, diagnosed only after ruling out illness. [24]
Active recovery Gentle exercise instead of complete rest. [22]
DOMS Muscle soreness that peaks 1–3 days after hard or unfamiliar exercise. [22]
REDs / LEA Health and performance harm from eating too little for the training you do (low energy availability). CAT2 is the IOC's checklist for spotting it. [29]
HRV Heart rate variability: the beat-to-beat variation in heart rhythm, used as a recovery signal. [57][63]
Bone stress injury Bone damaged by repeated loading faster than it can repair, from stress reaction to stress fracture. [43][44]
Single-run spike A run longer than your longest run in the past 30 days. [37]
ACWR Acute:chronic workload ratio: last week's load divided by your longer-term average. High values did not predict higher injury risk in [37]. [37]
Taper Cutting training volume before a race so fatigue clears while fitness stays. [45]

Reading the numbers: - RCT: randomized controlled trial, where people are assigned to groups by chance. - HR / HRR: hazard ratio, how much faster injuries happen in one group than another. 1.64 means about 64% higher risk. - SMD: standardized mean difference, an effect size across studies. About 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, and 0.8 large. - MAE / MAPE / RMSE: the average error of a device or formula, in beats per minute or percent. - p = 0.07: short of the usual 0.05 cut-off, so the result is a trend, not a confirmed effect.

Cross-dimension insights

  1. Your two swim questions share one answer: the swim is general fitness, not running fitness. Swimming transfers poorly to running (§1), adds little running gain on top of a plan (§2), and its heart-rate data is unreliable (§4). So the app should log swims with session RPE, add them to total load for fatigue and fueling (§2), and not count them as running fitness in race-feasibility projections.
  2. "Keep intensity" connects detraining, the taper, and the 80/20 rule. Fitness holds when intensity stays and volume drops (§1). That's also how a good taper works (§3). The 80/20 evidence says the easy part must really be easy (§3). Together they give the app one coherent message: protect a little hard running each week, keep easy days easy, and treat volume as the flexible part.
  3. The runner is the best sensor. Self-report beats or complements objective markers for overreaching (§2), effort ratings are the only valid swim load (§4), and age-based max heart rate is ±10 bpm off (§4). That supports the brainstorm's principle of personal baselines and runner-reported signals over population formulas.
  4. Show the evidence strength next to each warning. The weakest evidence sits under the most visible warnings (§1, §3).

Contrary evidence

The red-team pass ran on the four major conclusions. All survived, each with a caveat that is now written into the text above: - Swimming is a poor substitute. The challenge: it gave about half the gain of running in trained runners [1], and a thesis in untrained people found real transfer [64]. No head-to-head trial compares deep-water running, cycling, and swimming. - Single-run spike warning. The challenge: one exploratory cohort from one group, self-reported injuries, and a cut-off that is a category edge, not a validated threshold. The 10–30% band showed more risk than the 30–100% band [37]. Weekly increases showed a trend toward injury [36][38], so the app keeps a secondary weekly flag. - Mostly easy over strict 80/20. The challenge: the claim that recreational runners do better on pyramidal rests on one VO₂max subgroup [31], and an independent meta-analysis finds no difference [32]. Polarized beat threshold-heavy training in recreational runners [33], so the app guards against middle-zone drift. - Wrist HR in the pool. The challenge: one Apple Watch study showed good accuracy in water exercise, but not lap swimming [50]. The app accepts heart rate for swims only from a chest strap or goggle sensor [49].

Recommendations

Each rule below is written as the app's plain-language recommendation, with its "Why?" and the strength of the evidence behind it.

One missed run makes no measurable difference. VO₂max falls only after about 10–14 days without running [8][9]. Swimming gives about half the running benefit, or a third net of controls [1]. R4 Never convert swims into "running km." Show swims as their own sport in total load. The common 4:1 ratio comes from coaches, with no studies cited [17]. This research found no validated conversion. High (absence of evidence) UX: fitness view
R5 Return-to-run nudge after 2 or more weeks of mostly swimming: rebuild long-run distance gradually. Swimming builds bone less than weight-bearing sport [18], and unloaded tendons lose stiffness [19][20]. A single-run spike raises injury risk [37]. Medium (inference from unloading studies) PRD: body map / ramp
R6 Primary ramp warning: a run more than 10% longer than your longest run in the past 30 days. Secondary: weekly distance up more than 30% over 2 weeks. Label both "early evidence". Medium (one group, not replicated) PRD: MUST #5 (mileage-ramp warning)
R7 Mostly easy, not strict 80/20. Count minutes in zone (and say so). Flag easy runs drifting above the easy-zone limit. Polarized ≈ pyramidal training intensity distribution [31][32]. Threshold-heavy training did worse in recreational runners, though not significantly [33]. Counting by sessions triples the apparent hard share [34]. High (intensity distribution); low-medium (drift) PRD: MUST #4 (80/20 view)
R8 Easy-zone limit from the runner's own max heart rate, with an override from a measured peak (for example, from a race or a hill effort). Default the easy cap conservatively at about 80% of max until the runner calibrates. Age formulas are ±10 bpm off [55][56]. VT1 averages 85% of max heart rate in runners [58]. Medium (VT1 single study) Architecture: zones; brief: personal baselines
R9 See-a-professional triggers: pinpoint bone pain, pain at rest or at night, pain earlier in each run, hop pain, swelling, or a new limp means stop and get assessed. Groin, hip, or front-of-thigh pain means urgent. Missed periods or recurrent bone stress injuries mean see a professional about under-fueling. Clinical review [43]; clinician guide [66]; REDs consensus [29]. Medium (single clinical review; consensus details partial) PRD: MUST #5 (escalation)
R10 Muscle and tendon pain rule: pain up to 5/10 that's gone by the next morning, and not building week to week, is OK to keep training on. Never apply this to bone pain (R9 overrides). Pain-monitoring RCT (tendon) [42]. Medium (one small RCT) SHOULD: good/bad pain translator
R11 Fatigue check-in: ask about readiness, sleep, and soreness. Show resting heart rate trends. Show HRV only as a personal trend. When effort ratings are high, show them next to last night's sleep. Self-report is sensitive to overload [27][26]. Apple Watch resting HR is accurate, but its HRV isn't [57]. Sleep loss raises RPE [54]. Medium-high PRD: body map check-in
R12 Taper: 2–3 weeks, cut volume by 40–60%, keep intensity. Meta-analyses [45][46]; marathon data [47]. High SHOULD: race week / Taper Jail
