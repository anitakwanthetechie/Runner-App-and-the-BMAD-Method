# Sports science of cross-training and load guardrails for runners

**Decision this research serves:** Which training-science rules can runner-app safely build on, as plain rules with a "Why?" trail? Three questions:
1. Does a swim that replaces a skipped run maintain race fitness?
2. Do three extra swims a week help, or should the runner rest?
3. What evidence backs the 80/20 rule, mileage-ramp warnings, and the point to tell a runner to see a professional?

**Scope note:** these findings shape the app's *recommendations*, not medical advice. Technical terms and statistics are defined in the Glossary.

**How to read the tags:** each key finding carries *(Verified)* when an independent second source confirmed it, *(Single source)* when only one study supports it, or *(Unverified)* when no source was found. Strength of evidence, such as "one small RCT", follows in the same tag.

## Executive summary

**Yes, the app can give evidence-based answers to both of your swim questions. They are more nuanced than "swimming counts" or "swimming doesn't count".**

1. **A swim in place of a skipped run (your first question):**
   - **One skipped run costs no measurable race fitness.** VO₂max starts to fall only after about 10–14 days of *no running at all* [8][9][7].
   - **Swimming is the weakest stand-in for running.** In the only trial in runners, swimming *added* to a running plan improved a 3.2 km time trial by 13.2 s. Extra running improved it by 26.4 s, and the control group by 5.4 s [1]. So a swim is worth roughly half a run for running performance, or about a third once the control group's gain is subtracted. It mostly trains swimming [2][3].
   - **Deep-water running and cycling are better substitutes.** Deep-water running maintained running performance for 4–6 weeks in trained runners [13][14][15].
   - **Missing ingredient:** to keep fitness while running less, keep some hard running sessions and cut volume, not intensity [6][11][12].
2. **Three extra swims a week on top of all your runs (your second question):** they help general fitness, add only a little running gain, are unlikely to hurt, and count as training load, not rest [1][22][30]. The app wording is in R2.
3. **Guardrails for the MVP:**
   - **Mostly easy, not strict 80/20.** Polarized and pyramidal training perform the same [31][32]. The real risk is easy runs drifting into the middle zone [33].
   - **Spike warning:** warn when one run is more than 10% longer than your longest run of the past 30 days [37]. A secondary flag catches weekly distance up more than 30% over 2 weeks [36][38]. Both are labeled "early evidence". The weekly "10% rule" is unproven [35].
   - **See a professional:** a list of bone-stress red flags, such as pinpoint bone pain, pain at rest or at night, and groin or hip pain [43][66].

**Biggest caveats:**
- **Wrist heart rate can't be trusted in the pool.** Readings run 9–44 bpm low [48][49]. Swim load must come from your effort rating (session RPE), or from a chest strap or goggle sensor.
- **Several rules rest on one group or one small trial** (the spike warning, the swim-vs.-run trial, and the pain rule). The app's "Why?" text should say so.

**Decision for you:** where swim cross-training goes in MoSCoW. Suggested: SHOULD (sprint 2), with R3 and R4 in the MUST load model (see Recommendations).

The full app rules, with their "Why?" text and evidence strength, are in Recommendations R1–R12.

## 1. Swim → run carry-over

**Swimming keeps general aerobic fitness but transfers only partly to running. A single swapped run is harmless, and weeks of swaps need a different substitute or a gradual return.**

**Detraining (how fast fitness fades):**
- **Stopping completely:**
  - After 14 days without training, distance runners' VO₂max fell about 4%, but running economy didn't change [8].
  - Female middle-distance runners showed no change at day 5. Their VO₂max dropped by day 10, and their 2,400 m time was 21.5 s slower by day 15 [9].
  - In another study, VO₂max fell 7% in the first 21 days [7]. *(Verified across three labs.)*
- **Keep intensity, cut volume.** Fitness is kept when intensity is maintained and frequency is cut only moderately. Volume can drop a lot [6]. Runners who cut volume by 70% but kept some hard work held their 5 km time for 3 weeks [10]. Cutting duration by up to two-thirds kept VO₂max [11], but cutting *intensity* lost it [12]. *(Verified.)*

**How well swimming transfers:**
- **Trial in runners.** In 30 trained runners over 8 weeks, adding 10% extra load as swimming improved 3.2 km time by 13.2 s. The same extra load as running improved it by 26.4 s, and the control group by 5.4 s [1]. Treadmill VO₂max and running economy didn't change in any group. Only the extra-running group improved its lactate threshold pace [1]. *(Verified. One small RCT, testing swims added to running rather than replacing it.)*
  - An earlier draft of this research said added swimming gave *no* running gain. Checking the original paper overturned that.
- **Specificity.** Ten weeks of swim training raised swimming VO₂max but not running VO₂max [2]. A review concludes that swim training "may result in minimum transfer" and that cross-training "never exceed[s]" sport-specific training [3]. Modeling in elite triathletes found swimming "highly specific" [4]. *(Verified. Three independent groups.)*
  - Moderately trained people may still benefit from a different activity [6]. A university thesis reports that swim training improved running in untrained people [64]. *(Single source, seen only as a search snippet.)*
- **No replacement trial exists.** No retrieved study had runners *replace* runs with swims.

**Better substitutes:**
- Deep-water running maintained VO₂max, threshold, and running economy for 6 weeks [13]. Four weeks of deep-water running alone maintained 5 km time [14]. Water running and cycling both maintained 2-mile time over 6 weeks, with a small VO₂max dip in all groups [15]. A systematic review finds deep-water running has effects on aerobic fitness similar to land training [60]. *(Verified across three labs.)*
- A 2026 meta-analysis found no clear difference between cycling-only and running-only training, but warns the evidence doesn't prove the two are interchangeable [16].
- **Ranking:** deep-water running ≈ cycling > swimming. This is an inference: no head-to-head trial compares them with swimming.

**Swim-to-run conversions:**
- Ratios like "1.5 km swim ≈ 6 km run" (4:1) come from coaches, with no studies cited [17]. No peer-reviewed validation of any swim-to-run equivalence was found. *(Unverified: absence of evidence.)*

**Bones and tendons:**
- Swimming gives a weak bone-building stimulus compared with weight-bearing sport [18].
- With a fully unloaded leg, tendon collagen production fell about 55% by day 10 [19], and Achilles tendon stiffness fell 17% after 4 weeks [20]. Those studies used complete unloading, so they show the worst case. Swimming plus daily walking unloads far less.
- **Implication:** after weeks of swim-only training, ease back into running.

## 2. Extra swims vs. rest

**Extra swims are a small aerobic plus, not a running booster. Easy swims are about as good as rest for recovery. All swims count toward total load.**

**Added cross-training:**
- Added swimming gave about half the running gain of added running, or about a third net of the control group (see §1) [1].
- In another study, runners who increased training through cross-training or through running only improved 5 km time to a similar extent. The cross-training mode wasn't confirmed and may have been cycling [21]. *(Single source. Medium-low confidence.)*

**Active recovery vs. rest:**
- A meta-analysis of 99 studies found active recovery reduced muscle soreness (DOMS) with a moderate-to-large effect (SMD −0.94), but only shortly after exercise. It had no effect on perceived fatigue [22]. *(Verified.)*
- A swim 10 hours after hard intervals improved next-day run endurance in 9 triathletes. That swim was hard (20 × 100 m), not easy [23]. *(Single source. Not replicated.)*
- No retrieved study showed an easy swim harms recovery.

**Overreaching and overtraining:**
- The ECSS/ACSM consensus defines three stages [24][25]:
  - **Functional overreaching:** a short performance dip that ends in improvement.
  - **Non-functional overreaching:** recovery takes weeks to months.
  - **Overtraining syndrome:** months or more, and diagnosed only after illness is ruled out.
- No single marker meets all the criteria [24]. Key symptoms are fatigue, performance decline, and mood disturbance [24]. *(Verified.)*
- **What to monitor.** Self-report measures respond to load "with superior sensitivity and consistency" compared with objective measures [27]. In recreational runners, nightly heart rate, "readiness to train", and an HR-to-power index best separated overreached runners [26]. *(Verified that the two kinds of signal work together. The claim that a questionnaire beats HRV was disputed, because neither replaces the other.)* HRV evidence is in §4.

**Rest days:**
- No peer-reviewed rule sets the number of rest days for recreational runners, and none says whether a swim day counts as rest. *(Unverified: absence of evidence. Rest-day advice in the app is practice-based.)*

**Energy (REDs):**
- REDs (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport) is health and performance harm from not eating enough for the training done. It affects women and men [29].
- In 109 recreational women, each extra weekly hour of exercise multiplied the odds of being at risk of under-fueling by 1.13 [30]. *(Single source. Cross-sectional association, questionnaire-screened risk, women only, counts all exercise hours.)*
- Missed periods and bone stress injuries are screening indicators [29]. *(Partly verified. The full clinical checklist wasn't retrieved.)*

## 3. Safety guardrails for runners

**Coach "mostly easy" and watch single-run spikes. Send runners with bone-stress red flags to a professional. Taper 2–3 weeks.** The taper evidence feeds the "Taper Jail" race-week feature.

**Intensity distribution (80/20):**
- **Polarized vs. pyramidal.** An individual-data network meta-analysis (13 studies, 348 athletes) found no difference in VO₂max or time-trial performance [31]. A subgroup hint favored pyramidal for recreational athletes' VO₂max. An independent meta-analysis (17 studies) found a small polarized advantage for VO₂peak only in highly trained athletes, and no difference in recreational athletes [32]. *(Verified. The pyramidal hint is low-certainty.)*
- **Middle-zone drift is the real risk.** In 30 recreational runners, polarized training beat a threshold-heavy plan for 10 km time (−5.0% vs. −3.6%) [33], although the difference between groups wasn't statistically significant. *(Single source. Small RCT. Low-medium confidence.)* A trial in recreational marathoners is under way [65].
- **How to count matters.** Counting by session labels triples the apparent hard share compared with counting minutes in zone [34]. The app must state which method it uses. *(Single source, in elite skiers.)*

**Mileage ramp:**
- **Single-run spikes.** In 5,205 runners with 588,000 sessions, a run longer than the longest run of the past 30 days raised injury hazard:
  - 10–30% longer: HRR 1.64
  - 30–100% longer: HRR 1.52
  - more than 100% longer: HRR 2.28

  Weekly change wasn't associated with injury, and a high ACWR was linked to *lower* risk (HRR 0.75) [37]. *(Verified figures. One research group, self-reported injuries, 78% male, mean age 46. Not replicated. The 10% is a category edge, not a tested cut-off.)*
- **Weekly 10% rule: unproven.**
  - A 10%-rule program didn't reduce injuries in 532 novices (20.8% vs. 20.3%) [35].
  - Increasing weekly distance by more than 30% over 2 weeks showed a non-significant trend toward more overuse injuries (HR 1.59, p = 0.07) [36]. *(Verified, after correction: an earlier draft called this an association.)*
  - Increases of 20–60% raised short-term risk in half-marathoners [38]. *(Single source. Wide confidence interval.)*

**Injury baseline:**
- Novices get 17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours, compared with 7.7 for recreational runners [39]. Previous injury is the strongest risk factor [40][41]. *(Verified.)* This is why R6 and R9 should weigh more for novices and for runners with a past injury.

**Pain rules and red flags:**
- **Pain-monitoring model.** Pain up to 5/10 during activity is acceptable if it settles by the next morning and doesn't build week to week. In 38 patients with Achilles tendinopathy, this model gave results similar to 6 weeks of rest [42]. *(Single source. Tendon pain only: it is not validated for bone pain.)*
- **Bone stress red flags:**
  - pinpoint bone tenderness (the "most significant" finding) [43]
  - pain that comes earlier in each run or builds during it
  - pain at rest or at night
  - pain on hopping
  - local swelling
  - a new limp

  Groin, hip, or front-of-thigh pain suggests a femoral neck injury and needs **urgent** referral. High-risk sites are the femoral neck, the navicular (midfoot), the base of the 5th metatarsal, and the front of the shin [43]. The symptom list also draws on a clinician's guide for runners [66]. A 2025 international consensus exists, but its diagnostic statements weren't retrieved [44]. *(Single clinical review plus a clinician blog. Medium confidence: the retrieved review text didn't show every symptom listed.)*

**Taper:**
- The best taper is about 2 weeks: cut volume by 41–60% and keep intensity and frequency [45][46].
- Data on 158,000 recreational marathoners favor a strict, progressive taper of up to 3 weeks [47]. *(Verified. The marathon data are observational.)*
- "The hay is in the barn" has no source that quantifies when new training stops paying off. *(Unverified.)*

## 4. Measurement validity

**Use effort ratings for swims and the runner's own measured max heart rate for runs. Show heart-rate variability only as a personal trend.**

**Wrist heart rate:**
- **In the pool:** wrist readings in water were 17–30% off (Garmin −44 bpm, Polar −14 bpm on average) [48]. An older wristwatch had limits of agreement of −53 to +34 bpm, while a goggle (temple) sensor did well [49]. A third study found the wrist far worse than the temple [62]. *(Verified, but small studies.)*
- **Apple Watch:** its only water result (MAE 0.9 bpm) came from an aquatic treadmill, not lap swimming [50]. No pool validation of current Apple, Garmin, or Huawei watches was found.
- **Running:** wrist heart rate is reasonable. Apple Watch SE error was 3.1%, but individual accuracy varied widely [51].

**Effort ratings (RPE):**
- Session RPE is valid and reliable across sports and levels [53]. In swimmers it correlated with heart-rate load at r = 0.55–0.94 [52]. *(Verified.)*
- Sleep loss raises RPE for the same work (SMD 0.51 overall, 0.39 in athletes) [54], so a high rating on an easy day may reflect poor sleep. Athletes often sleep less than 7 hours a night, and the consensus advises an individual approach to sleep need [28].
- No source was found on heat or mood effects.

**Max heart rate and zones:**
- Every age formula has about ±10 bpm individual error (220 − age RMSE 9.8, Tanaka 9.2, in 4,043 runners) [55]. Women's measured max sits below the formulas [56]. *(Verified.)*
- In 1,411 runners, VT1 (the top of the easy zone) sat at 85% of max heart rate, and VT2 at 94% [58]. *(Single source, though large.)* This contradicts the 65–75% often quoted by vendors.
- The 3-zone model (easy below VT1, moderate between thresholds, hard above VT2) is the simplest practical model [59].

**Resting heart rate and HRV from the watch:**
- Apple Watch resting heart rate matched a chest strap almost exactly. Its HRV had 29% error and is fit only for personal trends [57]. *(Verified.)*
- Interpreting HRV needs context from how the runner feels: in one study it rose both with overreaching and with improved performance [63].

**Reading the numbers:**
- **RCT:** randomized controlled trial, where people are assigned to groups by chance.
- **HR / HRR:** hazard ratio, how much faster injuries happen in one group than another. 1.64 means about 64% higher risk.
- **SMD:** standardized mean difference, an effect size across studies. About 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, and 0.8 large.
- **MAE / MAPE / RMSE:** the average error of a device or formula, in beats per minute or percent.
- **p = 0.07:** short of the usual 0.05 cut-off, so the result is a trend, not a confirmed effect.

## Cross-dimension insights

1. **Your two swim questions share one answer: the swim is general fitness, not running fitness.** Swimming transfers poorly to running (§1), adds little running gain on top of a plan (§2), and its heart-rate data is unreliable (§4). So the app should log swims with session RPE, add them to *total* load for fatigue and fueling (§2), and **not** count them as running fitness in race-feasibility projections.
2. **"Keep intensity" connects detraining, the taper, and the 80/20 rule.** Fitness holds when intensity stays and volume drops (§1). That's also how a good taper works (§3). The 80/20 evidence says the *easy* part must really be easy (§3). Together they give the app one coherent message: protect a little hard running each week, keep easy days easy, and treat volume as the flexible part.
3. **The runner is the best sensor.** Self-report beats or complements objective markers for overreaching (§2), effort ratings are the only valid swim load (§4), and age-based max heart rate is ±10 bpm off (§4). That supports the brainstorm's principle of personal baselines and runner-reported signals over population formulas.
4. **Show the evidence strength next to each warning.** The weakest evidence sits under the most visible warnings (§1, §3).

## Contrary evidence

The red-team pass ran on the four major conclusions. All survived, each with a caveat that is now written into the text above:
- **Swimming is a poor substitute.** The challenge: it gave about half the gain of running in trained runners [1], and a thesis in untrained people found real transfer [64]. No head-to-head trial compares deep-water running, cycling, and swimming.
- **Single-run spike warning.** The challenge: one exploratory cohort from one group, self-reported injuries, and a cut-off that is a category edge, not a validated threshold. The 10–30% band showed *more* risk than the 30–100% band [37]. Weekly increases showed a trend toward injury [36][38], so the app keeps a secondary weekly flag.
- **Mostly easy over strict 80/20.** The challenge: the claim that recreational runners do better on pyramidal rests on one VO₂max subgroup [31], and an independent meta-analysis finds no difference [32]. Polarized beat threshold-heavy training in recreational runners [33], so the app guards against middle-zone drift.
- **Wrist HR in the pool.** The challenge: one Apple Watch study showed good accuracy in water exercise, but not lap swimming [50]. The app accepts heart rate for swims only from a chest strap or goggle sensor [49].
